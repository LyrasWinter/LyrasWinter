The First Pour

The sun's rays slip between clouds in a Baroque sky and reach through the wide window, to brush against my skin like a warm hand. My fingers curl around a ceramic cup and heat sinks into my palms as I sip the house blend, poured as a long black. Tucked into the café's corner, seated at a sandy wooden bench, I watch the world drift by. The scent of freshly roasted coffee beans fills my lungs and my eyes drift shut.

Life pauses.

For a moment, I simply sit; breathe, and let the smallest knot in my shoulders loosen.

The drive up through the Dandenong Ranges had been pure bliss. Shadows danced, cast by low branches which turned the road into a tunnel of foliage. Green and brown flickered like church-lit stained glass as I sped along winding curves. My favourite K-pop music blaring and the windows wound down, allowing crisp mountain air to fill the car.

In that moment, I had taken my first real breath in over a year.

Earlier today, I upset my aunt. Again.

After an injury at work, I moved back into the family home just over a year ago, after spending eight years away. My aunt, my dad, my pop, my cat and me — we all live under one roof; though dad is rarely there, working day and night.

I try my best, but our ways of living clash like mismatched rhythms. I've grasped one expectation, risen to it, only to stumble on the next. This morning was no exception.

Needing space to think, I got into the car and just drove, letting the universe pull me wherever it chose. And that pull had brought me here. To this window. To this warm cup of coffee. And here, in this coffee shop, no one knows me. No one knows how much of a problem I am.

Single Origin — El Boquerón

Tropical Fruit, Rose Water, Strawberries, Honey

A new day, a different week, I'm back. Stepping up to the counter, the barista gives me a lopsided half smile, which makes my heart give a tiny flutter.

"What'll it be?"

I freeze. I haven't rehearsed or even thought about my order. My head is still back at the house, buzzing, thinking about earlier today. My eyes dart to the menu and land on the most colourful blend.

"Single Origin," I blurt. "Long black."

I've always liked coffee but never claimed to be a connoisseur. Still, something about this choice feels right, a small act of colour on what has started off as a grey day.

I retreat to my same window seat, my companion for the day a book I've been devouring. Sunlight pools across the page and the world outside begins blurring at the edges.

"Would you like honey in your coffee?"

Startled, I'm torn from my book. The voice had come from behind the espresso machine. It's been a long time since anyone went out of their way to engage me and for a heartbeat, the old me surfaces.

"No thanks," I say, a glimmer of mischief flickering back. "I'm sweet enough."

He's heard it before — a thousand times — but when paired with my cheeky wink, it draws out the smile I'd noticed earlier.

It's midweek; only one other customer sits at the bar, a tall man chatting with the barista. The quiet is its own kind of bliss.

Looking up from my book for the briefest moment, I make eye contact with the tall customer and without hesitation he asks what I'm reading. My answer tumbles out too quickly. The theories! The ideas! How it resonates, what it's teaching me about my own mind. In my head, it's the perfect amount of detail to offer up to that simple question, but out loud, to most people, it's probably oversharing.

Panic flickers.

His eyes widen as the words sink in, the rush of information finding its place. Then, unexpectedly, he engages. Not just politely, but with genuine enthusiasm. The barista listens too, a glimmer of interest, approval even.

And just like that, a conversation unfolds between the three of us. We talk for over an hour about the sociology of people, the world, and the strange patterns that shape us. The customer leaves; the barista and I are nowhere near done. It's been an eternity since I've had a conversation like this, and it feels — wonderful. Enriching. Alive.

To the Moon

Dried Stone Fruit + Florals, Cinnamon, Cardamom, Vibrant Berries, Jasmine and Star Anise

I begin to frequent the coffee shop more often. I drift from the sunlit window seat to the end of the counter, where I watch the baristas at work.

From here, it feels like a dance, a choreography refined by repetition. The hiss of steam, the hum of grinders, the swift clink of cups set down just so, each vessel — glass, mug, espresso cup — its own note in the symphony. Each movement practiced, each accompanying gesture, adding to the rhythm of service. Between the rushes, conversation flows, and in the pauses, I'm privileged to glimpse their lives.

I meet the owners. The quiet masterminds behind the melody of the shop. Under their guidance, I shift — from someone who merely drinks coffee, to someone who understands it; who feels the art in the pour and the story in the beans.

One teaches me the journey from soil to cup. How the beans are grown, how they roast them in the shed behind the café. How they make and package the cold brew cans. One afternoon, he teaches me the method to brew the bougiest coffee atop a mountain.

With the other, I forge a secret handshake. One that most certainly does not grant me access to the staff loo, allowing me to stay a little longer.

She quickly becomes one of my favourite faces that work here. She speaks with such a quiet confidence, inviting me into her dreams of opening her own café. As she describes it, I can see it — the walls she hasn't yet chosen a colour for, the cups she hasn't yet held, the art still waiting for its place. Blurring, changing, but nonetheless still forming, the picture being painted in the air between us, spun from imagination. Then the joy: she decides to leap, and begins to strategise the path from vision to reality.

The more I come, the more stories we weave, the more aspirations we share. These conversations were some of the most stimulating I'd had in a long while, inquisitive and creative, while others were less — intellectual.

This contrasts to my time when I'm at my home, where the current game plan is trying to make myself as small and quiet as I can be, hoping invisibility might buy me some peace. But now to add to the stress of life, there's a court case looming: unfair dismissal, a botched medical report, my old company eager to wash their hands of me and the injury they are now insisting they didn't cause. Bills pile, rehab costs grow, and still, I'm told by my family I'm not using my time well enough, as I deal with this. They're disappointed I'm not becoming "something more."

I want to make them proud. My grandfather especially, the man I've always looked up to. But today I couldn't even stack the dishwasher properly. If I can't get that right, how am I meant to make an independent life for myself?

Latte Art

The café door creaks open and before I even step inside, he spots me. Like a wildfire, a blaze of light and energy, he flings his arms wide. Standing behind the teal counter, he bellows to the entire shop:

"She arrives!"

Something ancient in me stirs — an old confidence, a forgotten playfulness. Invisible strings tug me upright then pull me around in a gracious twirl, ending in an exaggerated but undoubtedly elegant curtsy. And, for a fleeting moment, I feel like royalty.

More importantly, I feel seen. Not invisible. Not merely tolerated — welcomed.

The Wildfire leans across the counter, mischief sparking.

"Sooooo," he asks. "What do you do?"

I stutter, unsure because right now I'm not really doing anything. Sure, there's fixing my back and now court, but I don't have a career or anything. And my hobbies feel silly and uninteresting.

"I… do art sometimes. But not really. Not like my aunt, she's the real artist in the family—"

He cuts me off gently, waving the end of that conversation away.

"That's cool. But what about you? Tell me, what kind of art do you do?"

I shrink, embarrassed. My creations feel awkward, uncool, not worth mentioning. But he presses me further and promises kindness.

"A dress," I admit. "Made of scale maille."

His eyebrows shoot up. "A what? Show me."

My chest tightens. What if he laughs? But I can't escape now. With trembling hands, I navigate to a picture of the dress on my phone, before passing it to him.

He stares, silent. My stomach drops. Then—

"Wow."

I blink.

"Wow!" he says again, louder, grinning. "I've never seen anything like this. It's incredible."

Before I can protest, he calls over The Strategist. She studies the photos, eyes wide. Her verdict is simple, certain:

"You're an incredible artist."

An artist. No one's ever called me that before. I've always said I "do art," but never dared claim the word artist. And now, hearing it spoken aloud, something in it feels less like a title and just by the way it was said, more like a truth. A truth that had been waiting, patiently, for me to accept it.

Jesus Walks

Caramel, Milk Chocolate, Sticky Toffee, Almond Praline, Stone Fruit Sweetness

Summer settles in, heat shimmering on the asphalt, cicadas droning in the heavy air. Another staff member joins the team; we connect instantly, as if we've known each other for a lifetime. Her youth belies the wisdom that she holds, each time we speak she displays an emotional intelligence that astounds me.

Devoted to her faith, she invites conversation that feels both grounding and transcendent — about life, now and after. While I don't share her beliefs, judgment never enters the space between us. There's only openness and the willingness to step into each other's worlds, even if only for the briefest moment.

The respect she shows in every exchange reminds me that such dialogue — honest, gentle, even when we don't agree — is still possible. Whatever I believe about the universe, about spirit itself, she remains proof that at least one angel walks among us mere humans.

By now, I recognise the faces that drift in and out of my coffee shop — huh. The regulars who carry their own stories, their own small rituals.

At first, it's just a smile of recognition. Then a passing comment, light, easy. Over time, these moments deepen, and as I begin to know each member of the community, they begin to know me.

The hobbyist who teaches me the quirks of vintage cameras, explaining how light leaks can become their own kind of magic.

The couple with the baby, each visit another milestone, their child growing before my eyes like the measure of passing seasons.

The woman who always arrives looking ever so slightly different. New hair, new tales, new husband? It's only on one afternoon that I realise after she walks in, then walks in again that I've been speaking not to one woman, but twins.

And then there's The Seer — eyes like galaxies, a way of drawing truths to the surface. She leaves the deepest mark. At first I avoid her — her questions are too sharp, too exposing when I'm raw. But eventually, I let her in. And though the pain she sometimes brings forward is hard, she also carries some of it away with her. Not for me, but with me.

The things of value in this life are rarely easy. They ask something of you: vulnerability, honesty, risk. And with her, I learn that sometimes the hardest people to face are the ones who leave you changed; lighter than when you began.

Long Black

Shadows creep along the floor, stretching toward me like spilled ink. Out of the corner of my eye, I see them pooling, thickening, climbing. Tendrils curl up my legs, cold and unrelenting, slowly forming into shadowy hands, grasping, then finally slithering their way up my neck. They wrap around me, choking me. They pry my jaw open and force their way down my throat. Thrashing as I gasp for air, I go to scream. No one can see me, no one is looking at me, all around me, the café is unchanged. People sip at their drinks. Conversations continue. No one seems to notice that I am drowning in shadows.

Reality turns dark, I can feel someone behind me — I feel her breath on the back of my neck — but each time I try to turn, there is only darkness, a looming, endless darkness.

With a sickening snap, I'm pulled back into my café. My art hangs on the walls. My favourite people fill the room. I catch my reflection in the window and shrink. I'm dishevelled, unkempt, ashamed. The Angel points at one of my pieces, laughing lightly, then leaning over, telling The Philosopher it's only up because they felt sorry for me.

On the other side of the room, The Seer studies my work, unimpressed, then turns to me.

"Fraud."

The word slices through me as if it's made of Damascus steel.

The room turns as a unit, a circle of faces surrounds me, pointing and laughing. Shame bursts through my chest, failure crashing over me like a tsunami, sucking the air from my lungs, as I wish for nothing more than my eradication from this planet.

Behind them, a figure emerges swallowed by a long black cloak. The only other distinct feature is a porcelain mask, cracked down the middle, mirroring a perfect copy of my own face. I've encountered her often. She haunts me, hiding in all the shadows, distorting reality, always pluck, pluck, plucking away so many precious moments of joy.

Some who have encountered them know them as 'The Imposter,' while others have been known to call them 'Syndrome.' Black shadows leak from her hands, dripping then lunging at me, starving for a memory. Needling inky tendrils, only a breath from my eyes now, I can feel which one she wants to take; me, seated here, at the same counter. I'm showing The Wildfire my art for the first time.

The shadows recoil abruptly, the tormentor interrupted.

A vibrant figure leaps onto the café's bar, crimson cape whipping in an invisible wind. Hands on hips, he strikes a flamboyant pose, then launches himself at the villain.

I can't help it — the moment's so ridiculous it pulls a laugh from me. A real, unrestrained laugh, something I hadn't realised I'd missed.

Invisible strings tug at my chest, pulling me upright until I'm in a matching cape, hair caught in a cyclone forming of shadows. Together, we wrestle The Imposter down. I reach out and wrench at the porcelain mask, cracking it further, crushing the lie beneath, tossing the shards aside. Underneath: a blur of shifting features flicker through every family member, all who told me, one way or another that I will never be enough. That who I am, at my very core, is not enough.

I cling onto the memory she'd tried to siphon from me: the day I showed my art, mortified, then so wonderfully surprised as I heard the words; "You're an incredible artist."

Because I am an incredible artist. My art does deserve to exist.

The Imposter crumples, collapsing in on herself as the shadows around reach a crescendo, a tornado spinning into nothing. The final face to vanish before me is my aunt's — twisted with malice, disgust, outrage that I too dared to claim the word "artist" alongside her.

And then it's over. My comrade and I standing in triumph, the hems of capes whisking around our ankles, applause meets our ears as cheers erupt from the innocent citizens surrounding us.

With one final announcement my teammate throws his fist to the air.

"No one messes with Phallic Man and The Gash!"

Tears fall as I gasp for air from laughter. The crass humour has tickled the teenager within me, my sides hurting. As the laughter begins to show signs it might die down, one small, but ever so clear snort I had no say in, brings us all right back into uncontrollable hysterics.

These moments, so crucial now it's sad. To laugh, especially like this begins to become so incredibly necessary to stitch up any part of my soul. To feel companionship. To feel community. To feel hope. A hope that a hero might save the day, or even more so — that I may become my own hero.

Single Espresso

Conversations I didn't know I'd been so starved of.

Beanie low, steam curling around him like morning fog. I pretend every time that I am not looking for him. Slipping into the room, aiming for an image portraying effortless grace.

The smallest moments became the purest ones. Tiny pockets of calm that felt achingly ordinary, and that was what made them sacred. To feel something so normal, so grounding, was its own kind of cure. That gentle pulse of excitement, the good kind of anxiety that hums quietly under the skin, the way a single moment could swell until the rest of the world drifts softly out of focus.

These moments — fleeting, uncertain, regardless of where they led — became the ones that made life feel worth living.

One degrading day, I confessed to him how my attempts with boundaries at home had gone so poorly. He listens gently, every single time, then says it with the ease of stating the weather:

"God, they're narcissists, aren't they?"

No hesitation, no debate. Just harsh reality.

Cold Drip

Home now mirrors the weather outside: bleak, grey, a chill that seeps deep into the bones.

Therapy has become a lifeline. My sessions are full of lessons — skills for communication, compromise, boundaries — as if I might stitch myself into a version of life my family could accept. If I can just get it right.

But happiness is scarce now. Even in my coffee shop.

My hobbies all cease, one by one. I stop doing art. I no longer write. I don't play my flute. Silence and invisibility feeling far safer.

This morning, another cut: my art was taken down from the wall. My aunt's piece; better of course, now hangs in its place. I told myself not to care. But it stung. Proof again that I was never enough. I'd dared to hope I might make something of my art, but was told an artist isn't a job. Only my aunt, the one exception in their world, was "good enough."

Now at the café, I sit unmoving, my usual cup untouched. Today's barista reminds me of the Valkyries, sharp-edged and formidable, protector of her flock. Approaching, she kneels, and for just a brief moment rests a hand gently on my knee. From her, this is new — forged softness. A protector revealing a nurturer.

"I'm here if you need anything, okay?"

I can't reply. If I open my mouth, I'll break. But this kindness from her. The one who would take on anything for her flock— there aren't words that would have been enough anyway.

Double Espresso

One agonizingly repetitive day, I sit hollow at my usual spot. He brings me my double espresso, then sets two glasses of water beside it.

"A bad day at home?" he asks quietly.

I nod. I have no words left. Another argument at home about my cat's safety has been the catalyst of today's misery.

Time passes, the café grows quiet, and the moment transpires, one where I'm the only customer, his colleague on break.

"Would you… like a hug?"

I comprehend the sentence, take it in and process the question. Touch is foreign to me. My family never offered it. My instinct is to refuse. But some fragile part of me whispers — yes.

Opening his arms, I step in.

For a moment, I'm held. Safe. My breath catches, then steadies as I match my breathing to his. My body turns from cement to clay; the physical pain of the stress softly ebbing away. And for a moment, the unbearable becomes bearable.

When I go to pull away, he tightens his embrace, as if sensing I'm not finished. So, another moment we stay, reality drifting into oblivion in an empty café, and for a just brief moment, someone else helps hold these pieces in place.

I forgot what it felt like to be embraced without condition. To be held, not hurt. I could have stayed there forever. Stepping from his embrace, I couldn't hide from reality forever. Carrying the echo of safety with me, I return to that place that feels nothing like the word 'home' should.

The Dark Side

Rich Dark Chocolate, Cacao Nibs, Roasted Nuts, Caramel

I pull into the driveway and nearly hit my cat. My worst fear almost realised in an instant, and something in me snaps.

The rest blurs. My aunt has friends over. My dad is there. My pop too. I beg them not to let my baby outside, not to endanger her again. I plead, cry, shake. They observe as I unravel. And no one steps in. They just watch.

My aunt calls me unwell; she tells me she only wants me to "get better." I tell her I am of sound mind, that this is about my cat's safety, nothing more. She warns she'll call an ambulance if I don't calm down and stop 'coming at her' as she puts it. She tells me how I'm always such a "victim" when I beg for her to listen to me. I plead and plead but my words collapse against her certainty. And while all this goes on, her friends keep working on their art. My dad walks past me to the bathroom, turns the shower on, and leaves me sobbing in the living room. A spectacle in a space full of familiar faces, yet somehow, I'm utterly alone.

When you're in distraught, visible pain and no one helps you. Worse – when no one even speaks or acknowledges you — something inside you breaks.

I have never felt heartbreak like that moment. Not grief. Not sadness. Something deeper. An intangible ripping from the inside out.

And I think, briefly, that maybe death would hurt less.

I don't recognise myself. I've never understood self-harm, never even considered it. But now I'm so desperate to show them — show anyone — how much I hurt. I'm desperate for them to see it. They aren't listening, so it's the only way. I want them to see the pain they're causing.

My arm is bleeding.

A disgusted look from my father.

Thoughts, that aren't mine at all, entering into a mind unravelling.

And then — a brush against my leg causing me to lower my gaze. Eyes the colours of the ocean gaze up at me, both wide and alert. My cat— my baby. The one thing I have left. She knows something is wrong and it's just enough.

I scoop her into my arms, shaking and bloody, climb into the car, and drive away.

I call my best friend, driving with no destination, cat silent and calming, still pressed tightly against me. I follow her instructions, and by the time we both convene at her mum's house, she's decided for me: "You're never going back."

Tomorrow, she declares, we'll collect my things together. Relief crashes over me that I won't have to face it alone. Tangled with guilt, disbelief, and a violent sickness that washes over me at the realisation that I'm betraying them.

My family.

"I hadn't realised how bad it was, I'm so sorry."

It breaks me from my spiral, and as it turns out, I hadn't realised how bad it was either.

The brain is clever that way. It hides truths and alters reality in order for someone in these situations to survive. The brain isn't built to survive abuse from those who claim to love you. The kind that rewires affection into fear, and turns a childhood into survival training.

What I've lived in, what shaped me, the situation in psychological research, it turns out, has a name: a narcissistic family system. A web spun tight across generations, patterns passed like heirlooms. In this type of family, every role is cast long before you can speak: The Golden Child, The Enabler, The Lost One – The Scapegoat.

"Aaron shall bring the goat whose lot falls to the Lord and sacrifice it for a sin offering. But the goat chosen by lot as the scapegoat shall be presented alive before the Lord, to make atonement by sending it into the wilderness."
— Leviticus 16:9–10

My role was written before I even stepped back into that house — to carry their sin, to be sent into the wilderness so the rest of them could stay clean.

My aunt. My pop. My dad. Their behaviours weren't isolated moments; they were part of the system's survival. Gaslighting. Triangulation. The constant use of guilt and shame. The tools of keeping the cycle living on and on.

And me? My fate sealed: to hold it all.

It makes a devastating kind of sense. The Scapegoat is never allowed to win. If I did, the system itself would unravel, the cracks would have begun, and eventually their flaws would be exposed to the world.

Learning this is like finding the rulebook after years of playing the game blindfolded. All this time I thought I was in a game of Cluedo — trying to solve the mystery of what was wrong with me. Instead, reality is that I've been playing Russian roulette. And the revolver has only ever been pointed at me.

It's the rule book I asked for, but definitely not the one I wanted. But with knowledge comes power. And now, I have one option I never thought I had. The only way to win the game.

I stood up, and walked away.

"As a pour-over, obviously."

I sit in the same seat as I did that first day, looking through this same window. Then, the sun had streamed in; today, the rain streaks the glass, everything outside shimmering silver. A different kind of beautiful.

Pen in hand, I write this story. Not as a sad tale of what happened, but as a love letter. To this space. To these people. To the kindness that carried me when I could not carry myself.

When I return after a few weeks of settling into safety, the season has shifted. The Philosopher, The Strategist, The Valkyrie — all moved on to their next chapters. It feels poetic, like the curtain closing on one cast, ready for the new. Their absence marking the end of a season for me, too. It no longer needs to be my coffee shop; it can simply be the coffee shop once again.

I never expected what I found here. I came through those doors just looking for coffee and a quiet place to breathe. Instead, I found people who softened the fall I was always destined to take.

Every conversation. Every laugh. Every cheeky wink. A hand on my knee. A hug in silence. These things wove a net beneath me. The landing still hurt — god, it hurt — but not as much as it could have.

Because here, I was included. Here, life had a semblance of normalcy. Here, I was reminded of who I am.

We grow up with the saying, "Blood is thicker than water," as if family must always come first. But what most of us don't know is that is not the whole phrase:

"The blood of the covenant is thicker than the water of the womb."

The bonds we choose, the ones forged in kindness, in laughter, in shared humanity. Those bonds can be — and for me, are — stronger than the ones we're born into.

Now with a small army of people behind me, friends who understand and professionals intervening, I have support in every area of life in which I was struggling. I'm not facing everything alone anymore, and while the journey of healing will be long, against all odds, life feels a lot more worth living now.

Stepping out onto the glistening, mist-kissed street, I bid farewell to this space. Not as a goodbye forever, but when I do eventually return, it'll simply be for the impeccably made coffee.

One I know — as always — will be made with love.

In the years that follow, we have our romances, our heartbreaks. We stay friends, our threads diverge and deviate, and time pushes the narrative forward. Whenever our threads aligned, we'd play our regular game, pushing the boundaries of what's considered "appropriate."

I remember the day I joked about your dead mum; you laughed (fewf.) Bestowing me the grand honour of, "only person on this planet who would get away with that joke."

Then… silence.

The space where our threads would cross, for you and me, no longer exists. Redundancy a bitter sweet creature for us both.

Maybe this is our end?

My phone rings.
It's 9:30 a.m.
It's Dad.
Dad is never awake before 11am—Something must be wrong.

But no. He's inviting me to a festival.

And, well—a free festival is a free festival.

I always run into someone at these things. But this time, the only person my mind can materialise is—you.

The daytime turns to evening and I've run into everyone—everyone except you. Was my instinct wrong? That little gut feeling earlier in the day must have been off.

The end of the night draws near. At the last second, the puppets pluck their strings. A quick tug, an urge to turn—our threads cross.

The clouds part. The sunset hits just right and the crowd of sweaty bodies separates like the red sea, seeming as if by divine command. And there you are—arms outstretched like the second coming of Christ, haloed in the pink-gold sunset light.

I don't think. I run into that hug. It's been a year, maybe? I thought our threads were done intertwining. We talk. We laugh. You mention coffee. Are you serious? Are we really going to catch up… for coffee?

Bad news hits, a phone call that was a punch to the gut, a mutual friend lost to his own inner demons, friends called to gather, a check in on everyone's mentality.

I'm very aware we might bump into each other, and I can't lie—This time I was looking for you. The moment does in fact arrive, we know it was always going to.

We hug and you call me "darling." It's jarring… but I don't hate it.

You tell stories to the group, but they're for me. You're not looking at anyone else. You smile more when I laugh.

When it's time to leave, you mention coffee again. This time, I believe you.

The coffee happens. We talk non-stop about nothing and everything. The hug goodbye feels just a drop different.

We start texting. And suddenly, we're back.

It's the funeral. A celebration of our friend's life, of how fucking funny he was. Eight years of shared threads woven with grief, chaos, and laughter.

At the end of the night, you ask— "Have you ever genuinely thought about having sex with me?"

Of course I have.

You tell me to message you when I'm leaving and as I do you stumble out of the venue. The next moment, one I didn't really believe would ever be on the cards for us was now in motion. Driving to yours, we know exactly what's happening when we get there.

It's what Sam would have wanted.

Eight years of tension—ignited. The first kiss feels like a moment ripped from fiction. The rest wrote itself.

You look at me after we've commenced act three of our performance. Guised as a joke, but an honest question lies beneath.

"So—you're not in love with me now, are you?"
And I answer—
"No. Of course not."

I'm lying. Because in that moment it's the right thing to say. Because in that moment, I didn't have the words, not yet.

Of course I love you.

But it's not the fiery, all-consuming kind of love. It's something else.

Eight years of droplets forming a river. Stupid moments. Chaotic ones. The rare kind of bond that survives bad jokes and worse timing. It's the privilege of watching the person you've become. Being so in awe of the core morals and values you hold—the ones that make you, you.

I will always care for you. Whether our threads intertwine forever or just for this moment, I'll be grateful. Every interaction weaves a memory into existence. I'll look back on it all with fondness—for every part of my life, all that builds me into me.

Life is chaos. The future will surprise us—again and again.
But when I look back, these will be some of the moments,

the moments that made me feel the most alive.

It feels weird.

If you asked me just a few years ago, would I ever write anything more in depth than a shopping list, let alone place it somewhere others might find it to read, I would have laughed and said you were mad. The dyslexic kid who failed English in school? Who failed school as a whole?

Never in my existence would I have considered that I could enjoy writing until a friend pressed me to join a writing competition with her. An experience I ended up loving so much I immediately entered several more that year.

It wasn't long until I purchased a journal to explore personal writing further and aim to practice mindfulness that I realised how much clarity I gleaned from writing. How much of an insight I got into my own emotions, alongside a space to express suppressed emotions. A space to step back, analyse and see the world from different viewpoints.

Writing gifted me with clarity into my own world. See situations, albeit many unfortunately grim, but also an awareness to change my circumstances, the relations that weren't serving me and most of all, myself, my own behaviors and limiting thought patterns and beliefs.

Journaling for me ranged from the mundane of life, work, quiet times at home, minor grievances, to hardships of the family I was born into and realising just what that meant. Learning to navigate, develop and sometimes let go of friendships, as life keeps moving forward.

But most of all, I realised I write constantly of love.

"Oceans"

Genre: Witchcraft  ·  Setting: Sewer  ·  Character: Betrayer

Earlier today you told me they were down in the sewer—the witches, that is. This town of God's children had no place for witchcraft. We devised a plan. You filled a backpack with supplies. I took the wood-cutting axe from out back. You've always been my sidekick in life. I've never doubted you for a single second.

Now you drop down into the sewer beside me with a putrid splash. You don't react to the smell. I do. It claws at my throat, forcing its way in like fingers. I gag, swallowing it back.

"Move," you say.

We wade through knee-deep muck, torches lighting the tunnel. Your torch flickers first. Mine follows. Darkness swallows us whole.

You step in front of me, a blue glow spilling from eyes like oceans—ones that mirror mine, that I've looked into every day. Rolling waves that have brought me comfort since birth now illuminate the tunnel.

I try to move. Nothing answers. Not a single muscle. The axe slips from my hand, gone in the sludge.

You open your mouth too wide. Your jaw unhinges slowly, stretching past anything human, like a snake's. The sound that follows isn't a voice—it scrapes, low and guttural, like metal bending, tearing.

Your finger rises. It traces from my brow to my chin, light as breath. When you pull back, your nail has lengthened—thin, pale, tapering into a needle.

Your eyes churn now, swirling like a hurricane where there used to be still water.

"I have to," you whisper. "Mama says you're too much like Pa."

The words don't land. They drift somewhere behind me, distant and unreal. Pa is gone. The creek took him.

"He hurt her," you add.

My throat tightens. I try to speak, to force something—anything—past the pressure building in my chest, choking me with a burning urgency to be free.

Like a painter poised before a canvas, you lift your hand.

The stiletto nail presses against my shirt, dimpling the fabric before slipping through. Skin splits—sharp, precise pressure, followed by heat. My breath stutters, catching on itself.

You keep going.

The pain blooms, bright and endless. The tunnel tilts. The blue light deepens, swallowing my world.

Your face stays close to mine. Your eyes hold me there, those oceans pulled into something violent and spinning.

My vision frays at the edges.

You push deeper, slow and certain, until the point rests against my heart.

For a moment, everything stills.

Your gaze never leaves mine.

Then you thrust the nail forward, and I take my last breath.
